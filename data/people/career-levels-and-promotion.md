# Career Levels and Promotion

Last updated by: tunde@nimbuslabs.io

This ties together three documents that each cover one piece of the
puzzle on their own: `salary-bands.md` (the pay ranges per level),
`performance-review-cycle-2026.md` (how you're rated), and
`hiring-process.md` (how new hires get leveled on the way in). This doc
is the connective tissue explaining how a level actually changes.

## 1. What a level represents

Levels are meant to describe scope and expected independence, not tenure.
It's entirely possible — and has happened — for someone to be promoted
faster than a same-level peer who joined earlier, and the reverse.
Tenure alone is not a promotion argument on its own.

### Engineering

| Level | Typical scope |
|---|---|
| E1 Junior | Works within a defined task with regular guidance |
| E2 Mid | Owns a feature end to end with light oversight |
| E3 Senior | Owns a domain area, mentors others, makes design calls |
| E4 Staff | Cross-team technical direction, the level `daniel@nimbuslabs.io` (Engineering Manager) came in at |

### Growth

| Level | Typical scope |
|---|---|
| G1 | Executes campaigns against a defined brief |
| G2 | Owns a channel or segment, writes briefs independently |
| G3 | Sets department-level strategy, the level `priya@nimbuslabs.io` (Head of Growth) is at |

### People and Finance

Both departments share a level structure (P1-P3 / F1-F3), described
fully in `salary-bands.md`. Scope generally follows the same pattern:
P1/F1 executes defined processes, P2/F2 owns a process area, P3/F3 sets
department direction — Tunde (People Partner) and Henrik (Finance Lead)
are both at the top of this structure for their respective departments.

## 1a. Why levels are shared across some departments but not others

Engineering and Growth each have their own level structure (E1-E4,
G1-G3) because scope looks meaningfully different at each step within
those functions. People and Finance share a structure (P1-P3 / F1-F3)
instead, reflecting that both are smaller departments where the
progression from "executes a defined process" to "sets department
direction" looks similar enough in shape, even though the actual work is
different. This shared structure is a practical choice, not a claim that
People and Finance work is interchangeable.

## 2. How a promotion happens

1. Your manager proposes a promotion, usually timed around a formal
   review cycle (`performance-review-cycle-2026.md`) but not strictly
   required to be — a promotion case can be made off-cycle for
   exceptional circumstances, it's just rarer.
2. The proposal goes through the same calibration process as ratings,
   across the department, so promotions stay consistent between managers.
3. People confirms the new level maps correctly against
   `salary-bands.md` and drafts the updated compensation.
4. Finance signs off if the new placement is above the new band's
   midpoint, same rule as a new-hire offer in `hiring-process.md`.

## 2a. A worked example of the promotion timeline

To make section 2 concrete, here's roughly how a promotion plays out in
practice, using a hypothetical Engineering promotion from E2 to E3:

1. **~6-8 weeks before a formal review cycle** — the manager starts
   building the case informally, gathering examples of E3-level scope
   the person has already been operating at.
2. **At the formal review cycle** (`performance-review-cycle-2026.md`) —
   the promotion case is submitted alongside the regular review inputs,
   not as a separate process running in parallel.
3. **Calibration week** — the case is discussed across Engineering
   managers together with everyone else's ratings, so a promotion
   doesn't happen in isolation from how the rest of the department is
   being evaluated that cycle.
4. **Within 1 week of calibration closing** — People confirms the new
   band placement and drafts the compensation change.
5. **Communicated to the employee** alongside their regular review
   outcome, not before — this keeps the promotion conversation tied to
   the full picture of the cycle rather than being a separate surprise
   earlier.

This timeline is typical, not guaranteed — an off-cycle promotion (see
section 2's "exceptional circumstances" case) skips most of this and
moves faster, but also skips the calibration step, which is part of why
it's rarer.

## 3. Promotion is not the only way pay changes

A calibrated "exceptional" or "strong" rating under
`performance-review-cycle-2026.md` can result in a pay increase within
your current band without a level change — see
`personal/priya@nimbuslabs.io/review-notes-2026-09.md` for a worked
example of an in-band placement change tied to a review outcome rather
than a promotion.

## 4. What this document does NOT cover

- Exact promotion timing guarantees — there isn't a fixed "up or out"
  timeline at Nimbus Labs, and this document shouldn't be read as
  implying one.
- Department transfers (e.g. an Engineering hire moving into a Growth
  role) — this happens rarely enough that there's no written process
  yet; talk to People directly if this comes up.

## 4a. If you disagree with a level decision

Whether that's your level at hire (`hiring-process.md`), a promotion
decision that didn't go your way, or a rating you think was miscalibrated
— the first conversation is always with your manager, directly. If that
doesn't resolve it, or if the disagreement is specifically with your
manager's own judgment rather than something you think got lost in
calibration, take it to People (Tunde) rather than escalating informally
through other channels. This mirrors the general escalation principle in
`general/code-of-conduct.md` — disagree in the open, with the person or
team who owns the decision, rather than letting it sit unresolved.

There's no formal appeals process beyond this today. That's a real gap,
not a deliberate design choice — it's on the list of things People wants
to formalize, it just hasn't happened yet.

## 3a. A cross-department example, worked through

To show how sections 1-3 connect in practice: imagine a Growth G1 hire
who, over a year, takes on running an entire channel independently,
writing briefs without oversight, and mentoring a newer hire informally.
That scope shift — from "executes against a brief" to "owns a channel or
segment" — is exactly the G1-to-G2 boundary described in section 1's
table. Their manager would build the promotion case described in section
2a around concrete examples of that shift (specific briefs written
independently, specific outcomes owned end to end), not around tenure or
effort alone, per section 1's opening point. If the case is approved at
calibration, section 4's alternative path (an in-band rating-driven
increase without a level change) wouldn't apply here, since this is
explicitly a level change — the two paths are distinct, not two ways of
describing the same outcome.

## 4b. How this document gets kept accurate

Levels and reporting lines drift — someone gets promoted, a new manager
comes in, a title changes for external reasons per the FAQ below. This
document is reviewed whenever `general/org-chart.md` is updated, since
the two describe overlapping information (who's where, at what level)
from different angles. If you spot a mismatch between the two — say,
this document implying someone is at a level the org chart's title
doesn't match — flag it to Tunde rather than assuming one of the two is
simply wrong; sometimes a title changes before the formal level record
catches up, and knowing which is stale requires checking with People
directly rather than guessing.

## 4c. What managers should and shouldn't do with this document

For managers specifically: this document is meant to help you build a
promotion case and set expectations with your reports, not to be handed
over verbatim as "here's exactly what you need to do to get promoted."
Scope-based leveling (section 1) resists being turned into a checklist —
two people can reach E3 by different paths, and forcing the table in
section 1 into a literal checklist tends to produce narrower, more
box-ticking behavior rather than genuine scope growth. Use it as a
shared vocabulary for the conversation, not a scorecard.

## 5. Common questions

**Can I ask what level I'd need to be to get a specific raise?** Ask
your manager. Levels and pay are connected but a manager conversation
about growth areas is a better starting point than reverse-engineering
from `salary-bands.md` numbers alone.

**Does a title change automatically mean a level change?** Not always —
occasionally a title changes for external/customer-facing reasons (e.g.
"Senior Engineer (Tech Lead)" for John Adeyemi) without a formal level
change underneath it. Check with People if you're unsure whether a title
you've seen reflects a leveled promotion or not.

**I'm at the top of my department's level structure — what's next?**
For the three people currently at the top of their branch in
`general/org-chart.md` (Daniel, Priya, and — sharing the P3/F3 top band —
Tunde and Henrik), "what's next" isn't a formal higher level within the
current structure; it's a broader scope conversation (more departments,
more company-wide responsibility) rather than a numbered level increase.
This isn't written down as a formal path anywhere yet.

**Does moving from an individual contributor role into management change
your level?** Not automatically and not in a fixed way — management
scope and technical/functional scope are evaluated on their own terms
each time this comes up, since Nimbus Labs doesn't have a strict
"management is always a level up" rule.
